01 — Mass

Understanding the amount of matter and inertia.

What is Mass?

Definition:

The mass of an object is a measure of the amount of matter it contains. It is a basic property of a body and is constant.

Mass does not depend on the object's location, shape or physical conditions such as temperature or pressure.

The SI unit of mass is the kilogram (kg). Mass is commonly measured using a beam balance or an electronic balance.

Remember:

If you take an object from Earth to the Moon, its mass remains the same. The amount of matter in the object has not changed.

Mass and Inertia

The mass of a body may also be described in terms of the amount of inertia it possesses.

Inertia

Inertia is the reluctance of an object to start moving if it is stationary, or the reluctance of an object to stop moving once it is in motion.

A force is required to overcome inertia. The greater the mass of an object, the greater its inertia. Therefore, a large mass is more difficult to start moving or to stop.

02 — Weight & Gravitational Field

Mass and weight are related — but they are not the same thing.

Gravitational Field

Definition:

A gravitational field is a region in which a mass experiences a force due to gravitational attraction.

Gravitational Field Strength

Gravitational field strength is defined as the gravitational force acting per unit mass.

g = F / m

The unit of gravitational field strength is N/kg. Near the surface of Earth, the value is approximately:

g ≈ 9.8 N/kg

This is numerically equal to the acceleration of free fall, approximately 9.8 m/s².

Weight

Definition:

The weight of a body is the pull of gravity on the body.

Weight is a vector quantity because it has both magnitude and direction. The direction of weight is towards the centre of the gravitational field. Near Earth, this means vertically downwards.

Weight has the same unit as force: the newton (N). It can be measured using a spring balance.

Weight = mass × gravitational field strength

W = mg

Key Idea:

Mass stays constant because the amount of matter stays the same. Weight changes because gravitational field strength changes.

03 — Density

Why are some materials heavier than others of the same size?

Definition of Density

Definition:

The density of a substance is defined as its mass per unit volume.

ρ = m / V

Where:

  • ρ = density
  • m = mass
  • V = volume

SI Unit of Density

Mass is measured in kilograms (kg) and volume in cubic metres (m³). Therefore:

SI unit of density = kg/m³

Other units may also be used, such as g/cm³.

Density and Particles

Density depends on how much mass is contained in a given volume. A material with more mass packed into the same volume has a higher density.

Higher Density

Lower Density

Worked Example 1 — Concrete

A concrete slab is 1.0 m × 0.5 m × 0.1 m. Its mass is 120 kg. Find its density.

Step 1: Find the volume

V = l × w × h

V = 1.0 × 0.5 × 0.1

V = 0.05 m³

Step 2: Use the density equation

ρ = m/V

ρ = 120/0.05

ρ = 2400 kg/m³

Worked Example 2 — Alloy

An alloy is made by mixing 360 g of copper, of density 9 g/cm³, with 80 g of iron, of density 8 g/cm³. Find the density of the alloy, assuming the volumes do not change during mixing.

Copper volume:

V = m/ρ = 360/9 = 40 cm³

Iron volume:

V = m/ρ = 80/8 = 10 cm³

Total mass:

360 + 80 = 440 g

Total volume:

40 + 10 = 50 cm³

Density = 440/50

= 8.8 g/cm³

04 — Determining Density Experimentally

How do scientists determine the density of different bodies?

A. Regular Solid — Example: Cuboid

1. Determine the mass, m, using a beam balance.
2. Measure the length, width and height using a ruler or vernier calipers.
3. Calculate the volume using:

V = l × w × h
4. Calculate the density:

ρ = m/V

B. Irregular Solid — Example: Stone

1. Measure the mass of the stone using a balance.
2. Place a known volume of water in a measuring cylinder.
3. Carefully lower the stone completely into the water.
4. Record the new water level.
5. The volume of the stone is equal to the increase in water level.
6. Calculate density using ρ = m/V.
Important: The stone must be completely submerged and no air bubbles should remain attached to it.

C. Determining the Density of a Liquid

1. Measure the mass of an empty measuring cylinder.
2. Pour a known volume of liquid into the cylinder.
3. Measure the mass of the cylinder and liquid together.
4. Calculate the mass of the liquid by subtraction.
5. Calculate density using ρ = m/V.

D. Irregular Solid That Floats — Example: Cork

A cork floats on water, so its volume cannot be found simply by submerging it and reading the displacement.

1. Measure the mass of the cork.
2. Attach a heavy object, such as a sinker, to the cork.
3. Measure the combined volume of the cork and sinker when fully submerged.
4. Measure the volume of the sinker alone.
5. Subtract the sinker's volume from the combined volume.
6. Calculate the density of the cork.
